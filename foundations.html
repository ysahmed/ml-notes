Foundations

The vocabulary every topic on this site quietly assumes — what a model is really doing, why it's always wrong (and by exactly how much), and the gold standard every algorithm chases. Thirteen ideas, three demos, zero math prerequisites.

1. The model of the world: Y = f(X) + ε

Before any algorithm, every ML course makes one assumption about how the world works. It fits on one line, and everything else is commentary:

The sentence the whole field is built on

$$Y = f(X) + \varepsilon$$

Y — the outcome you care about (house price, diagnosis, will they click). X — the inputs you get to see (size, symptoms, the headline). f — the true relationship that turns inputs into the outcome: powerful, fixed, and you never get to see it. ε — the noise: measurement error, luck, the customer's mood, the variables nobody logged. The part nobody controls.

The punchline is in that third item: we only ever see Y, never f. Every dataset is f's output with ε splattered on top. A model is therefore always a guess at f — a hypothesis about the machinery behind the numbers. The whole game is making that guess good on data you haven't seen yet.

2. Two jobs: prediction vs inference

Why build a model at all? Almost always one of two very different reasons — and which one you're after changes everything you'll care about later:

PredictionInference
The question "What will Y be?" "Which X's matter, and how much?"
What you want A good guess — the model can be a black box Understanding — the model must explain itself
The analogy A GPS: it just knows the ETA A mechanic: tells you why the car sputters
On this site almost every topic ends in a score Linear & Logistic regression wear their coefficients openly

Real projects often want both: a hospital wants to predict who's at risk and understand which factors drive that risk (because treatment decisions must be justified). Hold on to this split — it comes back hard in section 8 when we ask whether a model should be a formula or a blob.

3. Reducible vs irreducible error

Your model will be wrong. But it's wrong in two completely different ways, and telling them apart is the single most useful habit in ML:

Two kinds of wrong

$$\text{total error} = \underbrace{\text{reducible}}_{\text{your model's fault}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{irreducible}}_{\text{the world's fault}}$$

Reducible — the gap between your guess and the true f. Fixable: more data, better features, the right amount of flexibility. Irreducible — ε itself. No model can beat it, because the information simply isn't there. Even a perfect oracle predicting f exactly would still be off by ε.

The demo above is exactly this: the legs (point → curve) are the irreducible part — the readout literally shows it as σ, the noise size. The gap between the red line and the curve is the reducible part. Push σ to zero and only the reducible gap remains; keep the straight line on a curvy world and no amount of noise reduction will save it.

Why this matters for judging models

Two models can never be compared on absolute error alone — they both face the same floor. What you compare is the part above the floor: how much reducible error each one leaves on the table. And when a colleague promises "zero error," this is the concept that says they're selling something.

4. Three ways to learn: supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised

Everything above assumes you have a dataset. What's in it sorts all of ML into three families:

SetupWhat you're givenThe questionExamples
Supervised Inputs X and the answers Y (labels) "Learn the mapping from X to Y" every topic on this site (01–04)
Unsupervised Inputs X only — nobody wrote the answers "Find structure that's already in there" clustering, dimensionality reduction (coming later)
Semi-supervised A few labels + a mountain of unlabeled data "Let the unlabeled pile help the labeled one" label a few photos, let the model learn from the rest

The words are dumber than they sound: supervised = a teacher graded your training data. No teacher = unsupervised. Some students have answer keys, most don't = semi-supervised. Nearly everything you'll meet first is supervised, because it's the setup where you can actually measure whether you're winning.

5. Regression vs classification: the shape of the answer

In supervised learning, your target Y decides which of two games you're playing — and the game decides how you're graded:

RegressionClassification
Y isa number (price, temperature, demand)a category (spam/ham, benign/malignant)
You outputa valuea label (often with a probability)
You're graded byMSE — how far off were you? (§6)error rate — how often were you wrong? (§9)
On this siteLinear RegressionLogistic, KNN, Naive Bayes

One boundary case to defuse now: predicting "probability of spam" sounds like regression (a number between 0 and 1), but it's a classification model — the number is just the model's confidence before you pick a side.

6. Training vs test error — and the trap called overfitting

For numbers (regression), the standard report card is the mean squared error — average miss, with big misses punished extra:

Mean squared error

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

For each point: take the miss (actual − predicted), square it (so a huge miss counts much more than a small one), average across all points. Units are "Y squared" — a 20-point miss is 4× worse than a 10-point miss, not 2×.

Here's the trap. If you grade your model on the same data it studied (the training set), it can cheat — and it will. Give it enough flexibility and it memorizes every point, including the noise: training error slides toward zero while the model gets worse at its actual job. That's overfitting — the straight-A student who has never solved a problem he hasn't seen.

The fix is old-fashioned: hold back some data (the test set) that the model never studies, and grade it there. Test error is the honest estimate of "what happens on the next real customer."

7. The bias–variance trade-off

Why does the test error have a U shape at all? Because the reducible error in §3 has two ingredients, and they move in opposite directions when you add flexibility:

BiasVariance
Plain English Stubborn wrongness — the model's assumption is too simple and misses the pattern everywhere Twitchiness — the model changes a lot if you retrain it on a different sample of the same data
Feels like a too-dumb approximation, an X drawn through a curve a wiggly line that would look different tomorrow
Reduced by more flexibility, better features more data, less flexibility
On the U-curve the left side (degree 1–2) the right side (degree 12+)

Where test error comes from

$$\text{test error} \approx \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \text{irreducible noise}$$

The noise term is §3's floor and never moves. The other two are a seesaw: crank up flexibility and bias drops while variance climbs. The art is parking at the bottom of the U — and the slider in §6 lets you feel the seesaw directly.

Two honest notes: (1) you can't see either term directly — you infer them from train-vs-test behavior (left of the U: both errors high; right: train low, test high). (2) More data shrinks variance but never touches bias — which is why a bigger dataset can't save a model that's fundamentally the wrong shape.

8. Parametric vs non-parametric · flexibility vs interpretability

Every algorithm picks a side on a deep philosophical question: do you decide the model's shape up front, or let the data draw it?

ParametricNon-parametric
The moveAssume a shape (a line, a curve formula), then fit its few parametersAssume no shape — keep the data and bend around it
Trainsfast — you're estimating a handful of numbersslow-ish — the dataset is part of the model
Explainsbeautifully — "each extra room adds $12k"badly — "the 3rd-nearest houses said so"
Riskhigh bias if the assumed shape is wrong (§7)high variance with little data (§7)
On this siteLinear, LogisticKNN

"Non-parametric" is a misleading name — they use tons of effective parameters (every data point is one). It just means "no parameters you agreed on before seeing the data."

Flexibility vs interpretability — the practical trade-off

These two properties pull against each other on every real project. Slide toward interpretability when someone must approve or act on the reasons (medicine, credit, science — and your own intuition during exploration). Slide toward flexibility when only the prediction matters and the data is rich enough to fill a complex model without tripping into variance. It's §2 (inference vs prediction) showing up again as a hardware choice.

9. The gold standard: error rate, the Bayes classifier, and the floor

For categories (§5), the report card isn't MSE — it's brutally simple:

Classification error rate

$$\text{error rate} = \frac{\#\text{ wrong predictions}}{\#\text{ all predictions}}$$

6 wrong out of 100 customers → 6%. Nothing else to it. (Its twin, accuracy, is just 100% − this — see §7 of Logistic Regression for when one number isn't enough.)

Now the thought experiment the whole field is measured against. Imagine a rule that sees the true probabilities — the real P(Y | X) straight from the world — and always bets on the more likely class. Call it the Bayes classifier. It's optimal: no other rule beats it on average. But even it loses sometimes — the classes genuinely overlap, and in the overlap region someone must lose. How often the perfect rule is wrong is the Bayes error rate — the classification version of irreducible error from §3.

"Bayes classifier" ≠ "Naive Bayes"

The Bayes classifier is an ideal — it needs the true probabilities, which nobody hands you. Naive Bayes is a practical, deliberately-flawed estimate of that ideal (assume clues are independent, count, multiply). And KNN chases the same oracle from the other direction: "the local majority probably matches the true probability." Every classifier on this site is a way of sneaking up on a number no one gets to see.

Which sets the honest goalpost for the entire field: not zero error — the Bayes error rate is zero's final boss, and if your model reports beating it, check whether it peeked at the answers. The only race that matters is who gets closest to the floor.

The one-sentence version

Every algorithm on this site is one attempt to guess the unseen f(X) without memorizing ε — judged on data it never studied, chasing a floor (the Bayes error) that nobody is allowed to cross.
